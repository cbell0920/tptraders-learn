1.1 Trading is a numbers game, not a prediction game

Core Nobody knows what the next trade will do. You make money because a graded, rule-based setup, repeated many times at a fixed risk, wins more than it loses in R. Three things have to be present at once:

  • An edge — A-grade IOF zones, traded the same way every time.
  • Risk control — every loss small and planned (Module 2).
  • A process — every decision made during prep, never live (the MOPs).

Take any one away and you're gambling.

Example. A setup that wins only 30% of the time but pays 3R:

  • 100 trades × 1R = 70 losses (−70R) + 30 wins (+90R) = +20R.
  • At $100 per R, that's +$2,000, while being wrong 7 times out of 10.

Common mistake. Judging a setup on the last five trades. Five trades tells you nothing (Module 7.5).

In IOF. Every trade needs at least 3:1 to the opposing zone, so the break-even win rate is 25% (formula in 2.4). Being wrong most of the time is normal.

1.2 Orders: everything goes in as a bracket

Core Three order types cover everything:

IOF rule — the bracket rule. Every order goes in as entry + stop + target, all at once, built during prep (Module 3). After placement:

  • The stop only moves toward profit, and only when a written rule says so.
  • No early close unless a written exit rule triggers (SCRAM, Module 3.8).
  • Never move a stop away from price. If you're reaching to do it, close the platform.

Example. A 1hr MES demand zone, prepped at 7:00 AM: a limit buy at the near line, a stop beyond the far line, and TP1 at the next 1hr supply zone, all in one bracket order.

Common mistake. A "mental stop." In a fast move you won't execute it. If the stop isn't in the market, you don't have one.

1.3 Bid, ask and spread

Core Every price is really two prices. The bid is what buyers will pay you; the ask is what sellers want from you. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid, and the gap between them (the spread) is a toll paid on every round trip.

Spreads widen when liquidity is thin:

  • the first hour after the forex daily rollover (5 pm ET) and the Sunday reopen
  • the seconds around a red-folder release
  • the futures daily halt and reopen

In IOF. This is why forex stops carry a 15-pip buffer, and why a forex stop that has already been moved gets extra room if the trade will still be open at the reopen (Module 3.2).

1.4 What a trade really costs

Core Three costs stack on every trade:

  1. Spread (1.3)
  2. Commission — per contract or per lot, per side
  3. Slippage — the difference between where your stop triggered and where it filled

Always measure costs as a share of your risk. A fixed cost is tiny on a wide stop and huge on a tight one.

Example (MES). Assume about 2 ticks round trip per contract for commission plus slippage ($2.50).

1.5 Long and short

Core Long means buy first and profit if price rises. Short means sell first and profit if price falls. In futures and forex, shorting is as normal as buying. Every forex trade is long one currency and short the other.

In IOF. Demand zones are longs and supply zones are shorts. Both count equally. The trend decides which side you trade (Module 4.3), not a preference for one direction.

1.7 Leverage and margin

Core Leverage lets a small deposit (margin) control a large position. It doesn't change your risk; your stop distance and size do. What leverage changes is how fast a badly sized trade hurts.

Rule. Size comes from the stop and your risk cap (Module 2.3). Leverage never decides it.

Example. One MES controls roughly 5 × the index, about $32,500 at 6,500. A 1% index move is about $325, more than double a $150 risk cap. That's why size comes from the stop, not from "one contract feels small."

1.10 Practice before money

Core Paper trading installs the mechanics for free: prep, zones, brackets and logging. It can't install real emotion. So the path is:

  1. Paper trade the exact MOP routine, on the same schedule and at the size you'd use live. No do-overs: a missed fill is logged as missed.
  2. Graduate when all of these are true. (A proposal, based on a common industry bar, not a school rule.)
    • 2–3 months of paper trading and at least 50 trades logged
    • Positive expectancy in R
    • Plan followed on 90% or more
    • No daily stop hit in the last 4 weeks
  3. Go live small. 1 micro contract for futures, or the minimum units for forex. From there, the two progression gates (Module 7.10) decide when you add B zones and a second contract.

In the journal. Log planned and not-taken trades too. Missed setups are data.

1.11 The six gates

Core Every trade must pass all six. One NO = NO TRADE.

  1. The zone is on the A list (18+) from prep.
  2. Direction matches the ITF trend (futures) or the currency bias board (forex, Module 8.4).
  3. Range to the opposing same-timeframe zone is 3:1 or better.
  4. Size fits the risk cap at 1 contract / minimum units.
  5. Open-position and daily/weekly limits aren't hit.
  6. No resting order within 5 minutes of a red-folder event for the instrument.

Modules 2–4 explain each gate.

  • At 3:1 minimum, break-even win rate = 25%.
  • Bracket everything (entry + stop + TP1) during prep. Stops move only toward profit, by written rule.
  • Measure costs as % of risk.
  • MES $5/pt · MNQ $2/pt · MYM $0.50/pt · MGC $10 per $1 · MCL $100 per $1. Forex pip (USD-quoted): $10 / $1 / $0.10.
  • Stacks: futures D / 4H / 1H · forex W / D / 4H. Nothing below the entry TF, except 15m refinement and the post-red-folder candle.

  • Six gates. One NO = NO TRADE.

2.1 Survival comes first

Core Losing streaks are guaranteed. With 3:1 trades that win 30–40% of the time, long streaks are built into the math (see the Deep dive), not a sign that something broke. Your first job is to make sure no streak can end your account. On a prop account, the drawdown keeps the account alive, not the balance.

Losses also hurt more than the same gain helps:

DrawdownGain needed to get back to even
10%11%
20%25%
30%43%
50%100%
75%300%

Common mistake. Treating a hot streak as proof that the risk rules no longer apply.

Deep dive — how long can a losing streak get?

A rough rule: the longest losing streak you should expect in N trades is about ln(N) ÷ ln(1 ÷ loss rate).

Win rateExpected worst streak in 100 trades
30%about 13 losses in a row
40%about 9
50%about 7

At a 30% win rate, plan for 13 straight losses, and size so that's survivable.

2.2 How much to risk per trade

Core Risk is a fixed amount set before the trade, never a feeling about this one.

AccountGroup default
Prop futuresSized to the firm's drawdown: a target risk and a hard max per trade. Example (Tradeify Select 25k: $1,000 trailing drawdown, $600 daily loss limit): $100 target, $150 max.
Personal futures1% of current equity
Forex1% standard · 0.5% if one currency is NEUTRAL on the bias board · 2% max, only on a 20+ zone where both currencies agree

Why size to the drawdown on prop accounts. A $1,000 trailing drawdown is the real account. At $150 a loss, it takes six or seven straight losses to fail the account; at $300, it takes three.

Why a fixed percent elsewhere. Your dollar risk then shrinks automatically in a drawdown and grows in a winning run, with no willpower required.

2.3 The position size formula

Core Size isn't a decision. It's the output of a formula:

Size = Risk in dollars ÷ (Stop distance × Value per point or pip). Always round down.

The stop distance comes from the chart (Module 3.2), never from the account.

Futures, step by step (MOP-01):

  1. Measure the stop distance in points, entry to stop.
  2. Stop points × $ per point = risk for 1 contract.
  3. Contracts = max risk ÷ risk for 1 contract. Round down.
  4. If 1 contract risks more than the max, check the other instruments for an A zone that fits. None fits = NO TRADE.
  5. Never tighten the stop to make the trade fit.

Max stop distance for 1 contract (example $100 / $150 caps):

Example — MES. Stop 25 points from entry: 25 × $5 = $125 for 1 contract. $150 ÷ $125 = 1.2 → 1 contract.

Forex, step by step:

  1. Measure the stop distance in pips, 15-pip buffer included (Module 3.2).
  2. Enter the risk $ and stop pips in TradingView's position tool or your broker's calculator.
  3. Use the units it returns. Round down.

Example — EUR/USD. $2,300 account at 1% = $23. Stop 40 pips including the buffer → about 5,750 units ($0.575 a pip × 40 = $23).

Common mistake. Starting from "I want 3 contracts" and hunting for a stop that fits. That's trading your position size, not the chart.

2.4 Reward-to-risk and the break-even win rate

Core The lower your reward-to-risk, the more often you have to be right:

Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + R:R)
R:RBreak-even win rate
1:150%
2:133%
3:125%
5:117%

IOF rule. The range from your zone to the opposing same-timeframe zone must be 3:1 or better (gate 3). It also scores: 5:1 or better = 4 points, 3:1 to 4.9:1 = 2, under 3:1 = 0 (Module 4.5).

Example. 20 trades at 3:1, winning 35%: 7 × 3R = +21R, 13 × 1R = −13R, net +8R.

In the journal. The weekly scorecard shows your actual break-even win rate: 1 ÷ (1 + average winning R). If you keep closing winners early, it creeps up.

2.5 Why small risk saves accounts

Core Around 1% per trade, bad luck becomes survivable instead of fatal. Small losses also protect your decisions: a 1% loss lets you sleep, while a 10% loss has you making worse choices tomorrow.

Common mistake. Sizing up on "high-conviction" trades. Conviction is a feeling, not edge. The forex 2% tier is the only exception, and it's a written rule (a 20+ zone with both currencies agreeing), not a feeling.

Deep dive — risk of ruin

Risk of ruin is the chance a run of losses wipes out the account (or the prop drawdown) before your edge plays out. A strategy can be profitable on average and still have a real chance of ruin. The stronger lever by far is risk per trade: ruin probability falls exponentially as you cut it. A profitable backtest shows expectancy, not survival.

2.6 Open-trade limits and correlation

Core Trades driven by the same thing stop out together. The MOP limits stop you from stacking one bet under several tickets.

Futures:

  • Max 2 open, from different groups: Indices (MES / MNQ / MYM) · Metals (MGC) · Energy (MCL). A long MES plus a long MNQ is one index bet, so it isn't allowed.
  • Max 2 trades a day.
  • Max 4 pending orders. The first fill at the limit cancels the rest.

Forex:

  • Max 5 open, at most 2 per pair.
  • Max 2 per currency, same direction.
  • Max 4% total open risk.
  • Max 6 pending orders. At the limit, cancel the rest.
  • No counter bets. Every trade must agree with the bias of both currencies on the bias board (Module 8.4). One NEUTRAL side is allowed, at half risk. A side against the trade is never allowed.
    • USD BULL + EUR BEAR → EUR/USD short: allowed.
    • USD BULL → GBP/USD long: not allowed, because it bets against USD.

Common mistake. Counting tickets instead of drivers.

2.7 Daily stop and shutdown triggers

Core A hard stop for the day keeps a bad morning from becoming a bad month.

Group default: 2 losses, then done for the day. No exceptions, no "one more to get it back." (Example prop account: 2 losses = $300 max, inside Tradeify's $600 daily limit.)

Forex weekly stop: −4%. No forex trading until next Sunday's prep.

Shutdown triggers. Any one of these means you're done for the day: journal it, close the platform, come back tomorrow.

In a drawdown, go smaller, take fewer trades, and keep the same rules. Don't abandon the system at the bottom: that way you pay for every strategy's drawdown and catch none of the recoveries. To be decided Drawdown step-down rule, if any.

The journal tracks failures as rules broken: Over risk cap, Over trade limit, Not on prep list, Against bias.

3.1 A stop marks where the idea is wrong

Core A stop isn't "how much I can stand to lose." It's the price that says the zone failed. Two consequences:

  1. Structure sets the stop. It's never tightened to risk less money.
  2. It's set during prep, before price arrives, as part of the bracket.

Position size, not stop placement, protects the account (Module 2.3).

Common mistake. Tightening the stop so the trade "fits." If 1 contract doesn't fit your cap with the stop on structure, it's NO TRADE.

3.2 Where the stop goes

IOF rule. Use the first rule that applies:

  1. A swing low (swing high, for shorts) sits beyond the zone and the trade still makes 3:1 → stop beyond that swing.
  2. Several zones stacked close together → stop beyond the farthest one, if the trade still makes 3:1.
  3. Otherwise → stop beyond the zone's far line (Module 4.4).
MarketBuffer
FuturesNone. Stop placed just beyond structure
Forex15 pips beyond structure

Forex stops that hold through a reopen. The original stop already carries the 15-pip buffer. The risk is a moved stop, one already at entry or at the 1:1 level. Before moving one, ask: will the trade still be open at the next reopen (5 pm ET daily, or Sunday)?

  • Yes → place the moved stop beyond its level by the pair's typical reopen spread.
  • No → place it at the level.

Never widen a stop after it's placed.

3.3 Where the entry goes

Core The default entry is a limit at the zone's near line (Module 4.4).

Optional: refine on the 15m. For a better fill only, and only under these conditions:

  • During prep, inside an A zone already drawn.
  • Only the entry moves. The stop never moves.
  • Score R:R from the unrefined near line.
  • Close the 15m chart when done. It's not for finding trades or reading direction.

3.4 Where the target goes

IOF rule. TP1 = the near line of the next opposing zone on the same timeframe as the entry zone. A 1hr demand zone targets the 1hr supply zone above it.

  • That zone-to-zone distance is the range. TP1 must be 3:1 or better; under 3:1 = NO TRADE (gate 3).
  • Mother-bar rule. While price is inside a daily or weekly mother bar (Module 4.7), the mother bar's edge counts as opposing structure. TP1 can't sit beyond it unless the trade still makes 3:1 to that edge.
  • TP2 (runner) targets the next opposing structure further out. It needs 2+ contracts or units.

Context, not targets. Previous daily, weekly and monthly highs and lows go on your top-down sheet (Module 4.7). When one sits inside your zone it counts as MTFC for the confluence bonus (Module 4.5). They aren't targets on their own.

Common mistake. A target at a round number or at "about 3:1." TP1 is a drawn opposing zone, measured.

3.5 Size to the stop, never the stop to the size

Core Stop distance and position size are linked: change one, recompute the other.

  • A wider stop doesn't mean more risk; it means fewer contracts.
  • A tighter stop doesn't mean less risk; it means more contracts and more chances to be hit by noise.

Example. The MNQ stop on structure is 90 points: 90 × $2 = $180 for 1 contract, over a $150 max.

  • Wrong fix: move the stop to 70 points.
  • Right answer: check the other instruments for an A zone that fits. None fits = NO TRADE.

3.6 Managing a futures trade

Core Two stop moves, both worked out as prices during prep and written on the order. No judgment calls during the session.

1 contract: everything exits at TP1.

2+ contracts: half exits at TP1, half rides as the runner toward TP2.

TriggerPriceRunner stop moves to
1TP1 fillsBreakeven (entry price)
2Halfway = (TP1 + TP2) ÷ 2The TP1 price

Set a price alert at the halfway price, or let the platform move the stop for you, so you don't have to watch.

Example — MES long, 2 contracts. Entry 6,500, stop 6,490 (10 points × $5 × 2 = $100 risk), TP1 6,530 (3:1), TP2 6,560. The halfway price is (6,530 + 6,560) ÷ 2 = 6,545.

What happens after TP1RunnerTotal (1R = $100)
Falls back to breakeven$0+$150 = +1.5R
Reaches 6,545, falls back to TP1+$150+$300 = +3.0R
Reaches TP2+$300+$450 = +4.5R

Why one contract exits at TP1. One contract can't be split. Holding it for TP2 risks turning a 3:1 winner into a breakeven scratch, and that only pays if most trades keep running, which you can't know until the journal has data.

Journal exit reasons for the runner: TP2, Trailed at TP1 (trigger 2's stop hit), or Breakeven.

3.7 Managing a forex trade: the 1:1 rule

IOF rule (school trade rules, MOP-06 E). Forex exits use their own plan:

  1. The nearest recent high or low must be at least 1:1 away, or no trade.
  2. The opposing zone must still be 3:1 or better for the trade to qualify.
  3. At 1:1: take half off and move the stop to entry.
  4. The remainder rides to the opposing zone, or stops out at entry.
  5. Write the 1:1 price on the order during prep. No judgment calls live.

Example — EUR/USD long. Entry 1.08500, stop 1.08100 (40 pips including the buffer). 1:1 = 1.08900: take half off there and move the stop to 1.08500. The rest targets the opposing 4hr supply zone, which must be at least 120 pips away (3:1) for the trade to qualify.

3.8 SCRAM: your authority to shut a trade down

In a nuclear plant, a SCRAM is the operator's emergency shutdown: an immediate, by-the-book stop the moment a defined condition trips. Not a panic button; a procedure.

Core After placement it's hands off. The bracket decides. But you're allowed to close a trade early, before the stop or target, when one of two defined trips happens. Nothing else counts.

The two trips:

  1. Midday trend break. At the 12:45 pm ET midday check, a 4hr candle has closed past the trend's control point (Module 4.3) against your trade → close it now.
  2. Max-pain gap against you. A max-pain gap opens against your open trade (U3 against a short, D3 against a long; Module 4.8) → close at the open. Don't wait for the stop.

Not a SCRAM:

  • "I got nervous."
  • "It pulled back a bit."
  • "I wanted to lock in profit."

Those are fear exits. Log them as Manual, with the rule broken Closed early.

The one-sentence test. Before you close, finish this sentence: "SCRAM, trip ___, because ___." If you can't fill in trip 1 or 2, leave the trade alone.

In the journal. Trip 1 is logged with the exit reason Midday trend break, being renamed SCRAM. Trip 2 is logged as Manual + Plan followed = Yes + a note, until the batched journal update adds it.

3.9 The red-folder rule

Core Red-folder (high-impact) news can flip direction hard and stop out good setups. Red folders are step 1 of every prep: check the Forex Factory calendar for every instrument you trade.

Forex pairBoth currencies in the pair

Not in a trade:

  1. 5 minutes or more before the event, cancel every resting order on the affected instruments (gate 6).
  2. Let the release hit. Don't chase the first move.
  3. Wait for the first 15m candle after the release to close. A candle pattern or a COG (Module 4.2) on that close, at an A zone on the trend side, means go.
  4. Read the current trend (Module 4.3) and trade only in that direction.
  5. Price in an A zone from your prep list, on the trend side → place its bracket. All six gates still apply.
  6. No A zone on the trend side → no trade. Sitting out a red-folder day is always allowed.

In a trade: decide during prep, from the facts, not live.

  • Bias agrees with the trade and the stop is at entry → hold through the event.
  • Bias agrees and the stop isn't at entry yet (even in the red) → leave the stop alone and let the event play out.
  • Bias is NEUTRAL or against the trade → close before the event (journal: Red-folder close).

3.10 Other ways a trade ends

  • Futures flat time. At 4:45 pm ET (3:45 pm CT) cancel all pending orders and close anything still open (journal: Flat-by time). Prop accounts follow their firm's time.
  • Shutdown trigger. Any of the Module 2.7 triggers: cancel everything pending and stop for the day.

Manual exits that aren't one of the written rules are a warning sign. The weekly scorecard counts them, and the target is 0.

3.11 Failure modes to watch for

  1. Following someone else's call. Taking a trade because a mentor, a signal or the group posted it, instead of running your own prep and gates.
  2. "Looks close" syndrome. Treating a 17 as an 18, or "almost 3:1" as 3:1. Nearly passing means failing.
  3. Sizing the stop to the trade. Moving the stop so the size fits or the R looks better (3.5).
  4. The lower-timeframe override. Opening a chart below your entry timeframe and letting it change your mind (Module 1.9). It's also a shutdown trigger.
  5. The mental stop. If the stop isn't a live order, it doesn't exist. Never move one away from price; Moved stop is on the journal's rules-broken list for exactly this reason.

3.12 During the session

Core The plan is set before 9:30. The session is about doing as little as possible: watch for fills, check brackets, and move runner stops at their trigger prices.

  1. 9:30 am: lock. Orders, alerts and positions visible; only charts at the entry timeframe and above.
  2. Fill → check the bracket within 1 minute. Stop and TP1 in place? If not, place the planned stop now, at the planned price.
  3. Limits. Open positions or the day's trades at the limit (Module 2.6) → cancel every other pending order.
  4. Stop out. Log it. Second loss of the day → cancel everything and shut down.
  5. TP1 fills → runner stop to breakeven (trigger 1).
  6. Halfway price hits → runner stop to the TP1 price (trigger 2).
  7. Between events, walk away. Come back on an alert, not to watch.
  8. 12:45 pm midday check (15 minutes max): re-grade pending orders and cancel anything no longer an A zone; check SCRAM trip 1 on open positions; score new 1hr zones for tomorrow's watch list. No new orders for the regular session.

  10. Journal every trade the same day.

4.1 Candles: read them in location

Core One candle packs four prices for one period: open, high, low, close.

  • The body runs from open to close and shows who won the period.
  • The wicks show how far price went before being pushed back: a long lower wick means lower prices were rejected, and a long upper wick means higher prices were.

Rule. A pattern only counts at a zone, and a higher-timeframe candle carries more weight. A rejection wick in the middle of nowhere is noise.

4.2 Changing of the Guard (COG)

Core A COG is a clear shift in who controls the bars.

+COG (bullish)−COG (bearish)
Pattern3–5 red bars in a row, then a green bar3–5 green bars in a row, then a red bar
MeaningControl shifts from sellers to buyersControl shifts from buyers to sellers
Where it countsAt a demand zone, in an uptrend (quadrant III)At a supply zone, in a downtrend (quadrant I)

Rules:

  • Validation only. A COG confirms a zone; it's never a trade on its own.
  • The COG bar must close, and confirmation is the next bar opening. Never anticipate the close.
  • It needs location (an A zone), structure and condition.
  • Pick a side and ride it: +COG only in an uptrend, −COG only in a downtrend.
  • A COG never moves the stop.

It counts in the same places as the candle patterns: the confluence bonus, the B3/S3 signal, and the post-news go.

4.3 Trend: three segments and a control point

Core Trend is read on the ITF (futures 4hr, forex daily), on closed candles. It drives the trend score, the daily bias and the forex bias board.

TrendRequirement
UP3 directional segments: leg up, a pullback holding a higher low, a leg up breaking the prior high
DOWN3 directional segments: leg down, a pullback holding a lower high, a leg down breaking the prior low
NEUTRALControl point broken; no new trend yet with 3 segments

The control point is the swing that produced the last new high (uptrend) or new low (downtrend): the last higher low in an uptrend, the last lower high in a downtrend.

Reading it, step by step:

  1. Mark the last 3 segments on the ITF.
  2. Mark the control point.
  3. Price closed past the control point? Yes → trend is NEUTRAL. No → the trend stands.
  4. It stays NEUTRAL until 3 new segments form in one direction.

Example. EUR/USD daily lows at 1.0700 → 1.0760 → 1.0810 and highs at 1.0850 → 1.0905 → 1.0960, with the last high clearly breaking 1.0905: UP. The control point is 1.0810. A daily close below 1.0810 turns it NEUTRAL.

The control point is also SCRAM trip 1 (Module 3.8).

4.4 Finding and drawing zones

Core A zone is where price left a base so fast that orders were left unfilled. Zones are found and graded before price returns; once the session starts, nothing is added, rescored or moved.

1. Find the base. A balance candle has a body less than 50% of its full range, wick to wick. A base can hold several balance candles.

2. Check the structure. All three are required: an impulsive move in, one or more balance candles, and an impulsive move out.

KindDemandSupply
ContinuationRally-base-rallyDrop-base-drop
ReversalDrop-base-rallyRally-base-drop

3. Draw it (conservative by default):

ZoneNear line (entry side)Far line (stop side)
DemandTop of the base bodiesBottom of the lowest wick
SupplyBottom of the base bodiesTop of the highest wick

4. Clean up. Another timeframe may tighten the near line only. The far line never moves in.

5. Validate. A candle closed through the zone → delete it (and cancel its order).

Common mistake. Marking every pause as a zone. A real zone has a clean base, an impulsive departure, and a structure from the table above.

  • B = 15–17 — log it, don't trade it until Gate 1 unlocks B zones (Module 7.10).

Grade first, always. Scoring is the first thing you do with any zone. No score = no trade.

Rescore only on a touch, a break, a new zone or a trend change. "Looks better" or "looks worse" is not a reason.

4.10 The daily update

Core Futures: Mon–Fri at 7:00 am ET, 30 minutes max. Forex: Wednesday after the 5 pm ET close only.

  3. Delete zones a candle closed through; cancel their orders.
  4. Trend. Re-read the ITF. Trend changed → rescore trend on every zone.
  5. Purity. Zone touched → rescore purity.
  6. Range. Opposing zone moved or broke → re-measure and rescore R:R.

  9. Set orders. Bracket every A zone inside today's reach.
  10. Lock. No changes until the session ends.

Module 5 — Building and testing a strategy

How a setup becomes a strategy, how to test it honestly, and how to tell when it's working, decaying or done. The MOPs are the running example: a strategy written so completely that nothing is decided live.

5.1 A setup is not a strategy

Core "I trade demand zones" is a setup. A strategy answers every question before the trade happens:

  • Which instruments and timeframes?
  • What qualifies a trade, exactly?
  • How much is risked?
  • Where are the entry, stop and target, and why there?
  • When do I stand aside?

The test. Could someone else run it from the written rules alone, without asking you anything? If it needs your gut, it's still a setup.

In IOF. That's what the MOPs do. Every judgment call becomes a yes/no test: "zone closed through → delete," "under 18 → off the A list," "under 3:1 → NO TRADE." Even "looks better" is banned as a reason to rescore.

Common mistake. Calling a style ("I trade momentum") a strategy.

5.2 Required, optional, and triggers: keep them apart

Core Every rulebook needs three separate categories:

  • Required conditions — gates. Fail one = no trade.
  • Optional confluence — notes that may add a point or confidence, but never decide.
  • Execution rules — exactly how the order is built and managed.

In the MOPs:

CategoryWhat's in it
Required (the six gates)A-list zone (18+) · direction matches the ITF trend or the bias board · 3:1 to the opposing zone · size fits at 1 contract / minimum units · limits not hit · no resting order within 5 minutes of a red folder
Optional / validationCandle patterns and COG (validation and the confluence bonus) · MTFC · B/S EMA setups (context only) · VWAP, EMAs, pivots, fibs (notes only)
ExecutionBracket at the near line during prep · stop and TP1 rules · runner triggers or the forex 1:1 rule · SCRAM's two trips · the red-folder steps

Rule. Any gate NO ends the evaluation: NO TRADE. Promoting optional confluence to a gate means you'll skip valid trades and drift from the rules you tested. Demoting a gate to "nice to have" is how bad trades get in.

Common mistake. Adding filter after filter to "avoid bad trades." Every filter removes good trades too, and too many filters just fit the past (5.4).

5.4 Backtesting without lying to yourself

Core A backtest answers one question: is there any edge here at all? It can kill a bad idea cheaply. It can't promise a good one will work.

Minimum standard:

  • Rules written down before testing.
  • 30–50 trades minimum, across trending and choppy periods.
  • Log every trade: entry, stop, target, result in R.
  • Measure win rate, average R, expectancy, worst drawdown and longest losing streak.
  • Include realistic costs (Module 1.4).

How backtests lie:

  • Hindsight. In chart replay you can see the right side of the chart. Hide it and step forward one bar at a time. Score zones only from closed candles, exactly as in prep.
  • Over-tuning. Tweaking until the past looks perfect. Each tweak fits the past better and the future worse.
  • Cherry-picked periods. Testing only one clean trend.
  • Perfect fills. Assuming every limit filled. Some zones get front-run by a tick and never fill; count those as misses.

Red flags: a win rate above 80% · one giant trade carrying the result · no losing streak longer than three.

Deep dive — out-of-sample testing

Split your history in two. Build and adjust rules on the first half only, then run the unchanged rules on the second half. If the result collapses, you fitted the past.

5.5 Forward testing: the dress rehearsal

Core Forward testing means running the strategy live without real money, or at minimum size. It catches what backtests can't: missed prep, late orders, real fills, and whether you can take the fifth loss in a row without breaking a rule.

Rules:

  • Same size, same schedule, same instruments as your live plan.
  • No do-overs. Missed fills and early exits are logged as they happened.
  • At least 20 trades and 4 weeks.
  • Compare with the backtest. A big gap means a leak, either in the backtest or in your execution.

In the journal. Use the planned and not taken statuses so forward-test trades sit in the same dataset.

5.6 Changing the strategy: one variable at a time

Rules:

  • Change one thing per test cycle. Change three and you can't tell which one mattered.
  • Judge a change only on a full sample (20+ trades), never on three.
  • Write every version down with its results. Memory isn't a record.

In IOF. The MOPs are revised from what each week's journal shows (MOP-08), not from a single trade. The bias board's point values, for example, are starting numbers to be tested.

5.7 Strategy decay

Core Edges fade as markets change. Decay shows up as a falling win rate or average R over the last 30 trades, more failed zones, and deeper drawdowns than your baseline.

Response: measure first; don't deny it. Track rolling 30-trade numbers against your baseline. If they stay clearly worse, restrict the strategy to the conditions where it still works, accept lower returns, or retire it. Don't fix decay by bolting on extra conditions; that's fitting to the recent past.

Common mistake. Sizing up on a decaying strategy because "it has to come back."

5.8 One strategy at a time

Core Each strategy needs its own sample, journal tagging, review and testing. Master one before adding another.

In IOF. That's why the MOPs hold so much back:

  • Live now: IOF zones and GTRAPs (futures), with A zones only during the beginner phase.
  • Unlocked by the progression gates: B zones, then a second contract (Module 7.10).
  • Parked, with no rules written yet: scalping, overnight futures, counter-trend trades, pyramiding, gamma exposure.

The journal tags each setup type separately (IOF zone, GTRAP, Scalp), so they're measured on their own and never blended.

Lifecycle in one line: idea → written rules → backtest → forward test → live small → live full → monitor → retire when the data says so.

  • Forward test: 20+ trades, 4+ weeks, no do-overs.
  • Change one variable per cycle; log every version.
  • Decay: measure rolling 30-trade numbers. Restrict or retire; don't bolt on rules.

Module 6 — Psychology and discipline

The chart isn't your opponent. Your own reactions to it are. This module merges the repeated topics (FOMO, revenge trading, tilt) into one section each and pairs every problem with a mechanical fix, because willpower is exactly what fails under pressure.

6.1 Your brain is wired against you6.2 FOMO and greed6.3 Revenge trading and tilt6.4 Process over outcome6.5 Discipline is a routine, not a personality trait6.6 Patience: doing nothing is a position6.7 Burnout and breaksQuick ref — Module 6

6.1 Your brain is wired against you

Core Three built-in biases every trader has:

  • Loss aversion. A loss hurts about twice as much as the same-size gain feels good. You hold losers hoping they come back, and snatch winners before they're taken away.
  • Recency bias. The last few trades feel like the truth. They're noise.
  • Confirmation bias. Once you're in, you only see evidence that you're right.

You can't remove these. You can route around them with rules decided while calm and followed while emotional. That's the whole job of the MOPs: decide during prep, execute during the session.

Common mistake. Believing you can out-willpower a bias. Build the rule that makes the biased choice impossible instead: a bracket order, a prep list, a hard daily stop.

6.2 FOMO and greed

Core FOMO is the urge to chase a move that's already happened. Greed is its cousin after a winning run: "I'm in the zone, size up."

FOMO rarely kills an account in one trade. It bleeds it through many small chases that each feel too minor to matter. Five half-R chases a week is 2.5R gone, every week, with no single trade memorable enough to learn from.

Mechanical fixes:

  • Prep list. If it wasn't on the list before the session, it isn't traded. Journal: Not on prep list.
  • The 9:30 lock. After the lock there are no new regular-session orders, so there's nothing to chase.
  • No gate, no trade. A chase almost always fails gate 1 (not an A-list zone from prep) or gate 3 (no 3:1 left).
  • Fixed risk on every trade, win or lose. A winning streak is normal variance, not a signal.
  • Say it out loud: "This is FOMO, not a setup." Labeling the feeling takes away some of its pull.

Rule. A missed move costs $0. A-list zones come around every week; a blown account doesn't come back.

6.3 Revenge trading and tilt

Core Tilt is when emotion takes the wheel and rules start feeling optional. Revenge trading is the classic form: loss → anger → bigger trade to "get it back" → bigger loss → bigger trade. Each step feels reasonable from inside, which is why you can't argue your way out mid-spiral.

Tilt also follows wins. "I'm reading this perfectly today" is a tilted thought. It shows up as oversizing and loose entries.

The early warning: urgency. Healthy trading feels optional; you could take it or leave it. Tilted trading feels necessary. The moment you think "I need this one," you're tilted.

Mechanical fixes:

  • Daily stop: 2 losses, then done. No exceptions (Module 2.7).
  • Max 2 trades a day for futures, and the open-trade limits. Journal: Over trade limit.
  • Hands off after placement. The bracket decides. The only early exits are SCRAM's two trips (Module 3.8).
  • Cooldown after a stop-out (proposal, not a school rule). Wait 15 minutes, away from the screen, before touching anything. Your journal will show whether you need it.
  • Leave the desk. Physically stand up and walk away. Without new prices on the screen, the anger loop fades.
  • Same size next trade. Never bigger because the last one lost.

Common mistake. "I'll just trade smaller and more carefully." Smaller size doesn't fix the state you're in. Leave first, trade later.

6.4 Process over outcome

Core You control your process: what you trade, the gate, your stop, your size, your exit. You don't control whether this particular trade wins.

WonLost
Followed the rulesIdeal. Repeat.Good trade. The cost of doing business.
Broke a ruleDangerous. Luck rewarded a bad habit.Expected.

A rule-breaking win is the most harmful result there is, because it teaches you to break the rule again. Treat it as a near miss, not a victory.

In IOF. At 3:1 and around a 30% win rate, most of your correct trades will lose. If you grade on outcome you'll abandon a working system. Grade on process instead. The journal's plan followed field and weekly plan-follow rate exist for this.

6.5 Discipline is a routine, not a personality trait

Core People who rely on "being disciplined" fail on tired days. People with a routine just run the routine.

The MOP routine (futures day):

  1. 7:00 am — prep (30 min max): red folders first, then the daily update (Module 4.10). Brackets on every A zone.
  2. 9:30 am — lock. From here the session is about doing as little as possible: check fills have their brackets, move runner stops at their trigger prices.
  3. Between events, walk away. Come back on an alert, not to watch.
  4. 12:45 pm — midday check (15 min): pending orders, the SCRAM trip 1 check, tomorrow's watch list.
  5. 4:45 pm — flat, then journal every trade. The day isn't over until it's logged.

The journal step is the most-skipped and most valuable one. By tomorrow you'll remember the wins as skill and the losses as bad luck. Write it down today, before your memory edits the story.

6.6 Patience: doing nothing is a position

Core The skill isn't finding trades. It's waiting:

  • for price to come to your prepped zone
  • for the fill, rather than chasing a near-miss
  • for the target, rather than closing early

You'll watch zones you correctly skipped (a 17, a 2.8:1) go on to work, and it will sting. That's the cost of the skill.

Example. Three days of waiting, seven B-grade near-misses skipped, one A-zone fill for +3R. The impatient version took all eight and finished the week negative. Same market, same edge.

Don't confuse patience with fear. Waiting for your setup is patience. Skipping valid setups because you're scared is a separate problem, and the journal will show it as a pile of "not taken" trades that passed the gate.

6.7 Burnout and breaks

Core Burnout doesn't feel like tiredness. You notice it in your results after the damage is done.

Warning signs (two or three together = stop):

  • Trading when you don't want to.
  • Getting angry at normal, expected losses.
  • Skipping your routine because "it's not worth it today."
  • Feeling you need a win to feel okay.
  • Thinking about positions when you're off the charts.

Fixes, in layers:

  • A daily cutoff time. Futures already have one: flat at 4:45 pm ET. For forex, set your own.
  • One full day a week with no charts.
  • Permission to take an unscheduled week off when the signs stack up. A week out costs you a few setups; a burned-out week in can cost the account.

Common mistake. Trading through burnout to "make back losses." The losses are often a symptom of the burnout.

Quick ref — Module 6

  • Biases can't be deleted, only routed around with rules.
  • FOMO fix: prep list + 9:30 lock + gates + fixed risk. Missing a move costs nothing.
  • Tilt signal: urgency. Fix: 2 losses then done · trade limits · hands off after placement · leave the desk (15-minute cooldown = proposal).
  • Grade process, not outcome. A rule-breaking win is a near miss.
  • Routine: 7:00 prep → 9:30 lock → walk away → 12:45 check → 4:45 flat → journal.
  • Doing nothing is a position.
  • Two or three burnout signs together means stop.

7.4 R-multiple: the one honest scoreboard

Core Dollar P&L can't be compared across trades, because your risk changes. R can.

R = result ÷ initial risk. Risked $100, made $300 = +3R. Lost $50 = −0.5R.

R lets you compare MES with EUR/USD, a $5k account with a $50k account, this month with last month. It measures the quality of your decisions, not the size of your account.

Watch these, in R:

  • Expectancy = average R per trade. Above zero means an edge.
  • Average winning R against your win rate. The scorecard shows breakeven win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + average winning R). If you're closing winners early, that number rises toward your actual win rate.

Common mistake. Tracking win rate and dollars only. A 70% win rate with +0.3R wins and −1.5R losses loses money.

7.5 Sample size: when patterns mean anything

Core Small samples lie.

TradesWhat you can say
Under 20Nothing. Don't change the strategy.
20–50Hints. Adjust one variable at a time, keep logging.
100+Real conclusions: keep, restrict or retire a setup.

The danger zone is the first couple of dozen trades. A normal bad run looks exactly like a broken system, and that's when people quit good setups. At a 30% win rate, 10 losses in a row inside your first 20 trades is entirely possible.

In the journal. The two progression gates in 7.10 are built on these sample sizes. The Review page counts your trades toward each gate.

7.6 Mining the data for your edge

After 30–50 trades, group your results and look for the spread:

  • By setup (Type, B/S setup) — which one actually pays in R?
  • By zone grade — A zones against everything else. The scorecard already splits these.
  • By session / time of day.
  • By instrument.
  • By emotion (once tagged) — calm and confident trades usually cluster positive; FOMO, revenge and bored trades cluster negative. Once the data shows it, the rule writes itself: if I'm tagging FOMO, I don't enter.
  • By rule broken — which broken rule costs the most R?

The subtraction test. If you stopped doing X, would your results get meaningfully worse? If not, X isn't your edge, however much you like it.

Plan-follow rate. If it's below about 80%, your problem isn't the strategy.

7.7 The weekly review (MOP-08)

7.8 From journal to plan

Core The journal feeds the plan, and the plan feeds the journal. After 50–100 honest trades, every rule in your one-page plan should trace back to a number:

7.10 The progression gates: earning B trades and more size

Core New live traders start narrow and widen only when the journal shows they're ready. Everyone starts at the same place:

  • A-grade zones only (Module 4.5)
  • 1 micro contract, all out at TP1 (Module 3.6)

There are two gates. You review yourself at each one, honestly, using the journal numbers.

Gate 1Gate 2
When20 closed A-grade trades40 closed graded trades in total (A and B both count, including your first 20)
ChecksPlan followed on 90%+ (18 of 20) · Daily stop never broken (no trade after the 2nd loss) in the last 4 weeksPlan followed on 90%+ · Daily stop never broken in the last 4 weeks · Positive expectancy in R across all 40 · B-grade trades positive in R on their own
UnlocksB-grade zones (15–17) alongside AFutures: one more contract (2 total, still inside your max risk per trade), which turns on the runner sequence (Module 3.6). Forex already sizes by risk and uses the 1:1 rule from the start.

Why Gate 1 checks only discipline. Twenty trades is too few to judge results. With 3:1 trades that win about 30% of the time, a trader with a real edge can easily be negative after 20 trades by luck alone. What 20 trades can show is whether you follow the rules. Results get judged at 40.

If a gate isn't passed:

  1. Name the one biggest leak from the journal, such as a rule broken most often or a session that loses.
  2. Write one yes/no rule to fix it (Module 7.7).
  3. Take 20 more trades, then run that gate's review again.

If B trades are negative at Gate 2, go back to A-only, even if you pass Gate 2 otherwise. Your contract unlock still stands, because it's earned on the whole set. You can try B again after the next 20 trades.

Not unlocked by either gate: overnight futures, counter-trend trades, scalping, pyramiding, and size beyond 2 contracts. To be decided When and how those unlock.

Zones

  • Base: balance candles (body under 50% of range). Structure: impulse in → base → impulse out. RBR / DBD = continuation; DBR / RBD = reversal.
  • Demand: near line = top of the base bodies · far line = lowest wick. Supply: near line = bottom of the bodies · far line = highest wick.
  • Another timeframe may tighten the near line only. A candle closed through the zone = delete.

Trend

UP / DOWN = 3 segments, with the last leg breaking the prior high/low. A close past the control point (last HL in an uptrend, last LH in a downtrend) = NEUTRAL until 3 new segments form.

Building the order

  • Entry: limit at the near line. Optional 15m refinement in prep; R:R from the unrefined line.
  • Stop, first that applies: swing beyond the zone (still 3:1) → farthest stacked zone (still 3:1) → beyond the far line. Futures: no buffer · forex: 15 pips.
  • TP1: near line of the next opposing same-timeframe zone, 3:1+. Mother-bar edge counts as opposing structure.
  • Bracket entry + stop + TP1 together. Stops only move toward profit, by written rule.

Managing the trade

  • Futures, 1 contract: all out at TP1.
  • Futures, 2+ contracts: half at TP1 → runner stop to breakeven → at the halfway price, runner stop to TP1 → the rest at TP2.
  • Forex (1:1 rule): nearest recent high/low at least 1:1 away · at 1:1 take half off and move the stop to entry · the rest rides to the opposing zone.
  • SCRAM (the only early exits): 1 at the 12:45 check, a 4hr candle has closed past the control point against you · 2 a max-pain gap opens against you → close at the open. Anything else = Manual + Closed early.

Red folder

Not in a trade: cancel resting orders 5+ min before · don't chase · wait for the first 15m close · a pattern or COG at an A zone on the trend side = go, with all six gates · nothing on the trend side = no trade. In a trade (decided in prep): bias agrees and stop at entry → hold · bias agrees, stop not at entry → leave it alone · bias NEUTRAL or against → close before the event.

Gaps (Sunday open vs Friday's range)

Novice gap (U1, D1) → no new trades in its direction · pro gap with the macro (U2, D2) → keep with-trend orders · max-pain gap (U3, D3) against you → SCRAM trip 2 · never chase a gap.

Progression gates (self-review)

  • Start: A zones only · 1 micro contract, all out at TP1.
  • Gate 1 — 20 closed A trades: plan followed on 90%+ · daily stop never broken in the last 4 weeks → unlock B zones.
  • Gate 2 — 40 closed graded trades (A + B): the Gate 1 checks plus positive expectancy in R plus B trades positive in R → unlock a 2nd futures contract. B negative means back to A only.
  • Not passed: one leak, one rule, 20 more trades, review again.
