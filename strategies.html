Adding a strategy

A strategy belongs here when it passes six yes/no checks:

  1. Zone. The entry happens at a supply or demand zone.
  2. Trend. It trades with the ITF trend, or it's a counter-trend B trade after Gate 1.
  3. Timeframes. It uses only charts at or above the entry timeframe.
  4. Prep. The order can be bracketed during prep, with no live trigger.
  5. Target. The target is opposing structure.
  6. No indicator calls. Indicators can be notes, never the decision.

Which side to trade: range and trend

Under review

The idea. Before you look for a zone, know which side you're on. Two questions decide it: where is price in its higher-timeframe range, and which way is the trend?

The decisions.

  • High in the range (upper 25%): sell.
  • Low in the range (lower 25%): buy.
  • Middle of the range, uptrend: buy.
  • Middle of the range, downtrend: sell.
  • No trend: sit on your hands. No trade is a position too. This is a habit, not a hard rule: a NEUTRAL trend scores 2 instead of 4, which keeps most of these zones off the A list anyway.
  • Counter-trend or sideways trades are possible, with a shorter target: 1:1. Take half off there and move the stop to entry; the other half, the dream team, rides to the opposing zone (Module 3.7).

How to read it. Range comes from the HTF chart, split into lower 25%, middle 50% and upper 25% (Module 4.5). Trend comes from the ITF chart: three segments and a control point (Module 4.3).

Do it Write the side down for each market during Sunday prep, before you mark a single zone.

Supply and demand zones

Under review

The idea. Big orders leave footprints. When price leaves a base so fast that orders are left unfilled, that base becomes a zone, and price often reacts when it comes back. You find the zone, set the order, and wait for price to come back to you.

The steps.

  1. Find the zone: impulse in, balance candles, impulse out (Module 4.4).
  2. Score it during prep. Only A zones (18+) make the list (Module 4.5).
  3. Run the six gates (Module 1.11).
  4. Bracket it in prep: limit at the near line, stop on structure beyond the zone, TP1 at the opposing zone, 3:1 or better (Module 3.2).
  5. Wait. If price never comes back, there's no trade, and that's fine. Once it fills, hands off: the runner moves at its set prices and SCRAM is the only early exit (Module 3.8).

What makes it work.

  • The order is placed before price arrives, so there's nothing to chase and nothing to decide live.
  • Fresh zones beat tired ones. Purity scores it: untouched 4, one shallow touch 2, more 0.
  • Structure defines "wrong." The stop sits where the zone has failed, not at a random distance.
  • Equal highs or lows just beyond a zone are worth a note: the stops resting there can pull price into your zone. A note only, never a point (Module 4.5).

Where it fails. Marking every pause as a zone, trading tired zones, and taking zones against the trend. The scorecard catches all three.

Do it Sunday and 7:00 am prep on the Daily Checklist.

Top-down alignment

Under review

The idea. One chart gives one view; three charts give context. The best zones sit inside each other: a small entry zone on the LTF, inside a zone on the ITF, inside a zone on the HTF. When all three line up, many traders on many timeframes want the same price.

The steps.

  1. HTF (range): where is price inside its range: lower 25%, middle or upper 25% (Module 4.5)? Mark the big zones.
  2. ITF (trend): three segments and a control point. UP, DOWN or NEUTRAL (Module 4.3). Mark the zones inside the HTF zones.
  3. LTF (entry): find the A zone that sits inside both, on the trend side (quadrant I or III, Module 4.6).
  4. Bracket it at the LTF zone's near line. Never open a chart below the entry timeframe (Module 1.9).

Why the small zone. The LTF zone is the tightest part of the stack, so the stop is shorter and the R:R is bigger, while the bigger zones around it supply the reason it should hold. A higher-timeframe high or low inside your zone also earns the confluence bonus (MTFC).

Where it fails. When the higher chart is unclear and you trade it anyway. A NEUTRAL trend scores 2 instead of 4, which usually keeps the zone off the A list.

Trend pullback

Under review

The idea. In an uptrend, the dip is the gift: it brings price back down to where buyers already showed up. Buy the dip into demand; in a downtrend, sell the rally into supply.

The steps.

  1. The ITF trend is UP (or DOWN for shorts) (Module 4.3).
  2. Mark where the pullback should end: an A demand zone (supply, for shorts), quadrant III (or I) (Module 4.6).
  3. Bracket it at the near line in prep, stop on structure. The control point below is what makes the whole uptrend wrong (Module 3.2).
  4. TP1 at the opposing zone, 3:1 or better. With 2+ contracts: break-even when TP1 fills, then the runner's stop to TP1 at the halfway price (Module 3.6).

What makes it work. You're buying where the trend's buyers already proved themselves, in the direction the market is already going. A candle pattern at the zone adds the confluence bonus, but the order is already waiting; you don't need the candle to get in.

Where it fails. A pullback that's really the start of a reversal. The check: a candle closing past the control point turns the trend NEUTRAL, and that's SCRAM trip 1 for an open trade (Module 3.8).

Wyckoff calls this a test in trend: a pullback during markup (or markdown, for shorts), often out of a re-accumulation pause. See Module 4.13.

Do it A zones on the trend side go on the trade card.

Breakout retest

Under review

The idea. When price breaks out of a range, the move is real, but the breakout candle is the worst place to get in: you buy the top of the move with the stop far away. The base that launched the break is a fresh zone. Mark it, set the order, and let the pullback come to you.

The steps.

  1. Price breaks out of a range with a strong move. Find the base right before the break: that's a rally-base-rally demand zone (drop-base-drop supply, for a break down) (Module 4.4).
  2. Score it. A breakout that clears the range usually scores well on Strength, and the zone is untouched, so Purity scores 4 (Module 4.5).
  3. Bracket it at the near line in prep, stop on structure below the base, TP1 at the opposing zone, 3:1 or better (Module 3.2).
  4. Wait. No entry on the breakout candle. If price never pulls back, the move happened without you, and that's fine.

What makes it work. Orders left behind at the base pull price back, and the zone gives you the same direction as the breakout with a tight stop. A break in the direction of the ITF trend is a with-trend A trade; a break against it is counter-trend.

Where it fails. A breakout that falls straight back into the range. The stop below the base handles it. Also skip breaks from tiny ranges in the middle of nowhere: the HTF range score keeps most of those off the A list.

Wyckoff calls this the test after the breakout: the last point of support (LPS), or the back-up to the edge of the creek (BUEC). See Module 4.13.

Do it Breakout bases get scored with every other zone during 7:00 am prep.

Range edges

Under review

The idea. When there's no trend, price swings between a ceiling and a floor. The edges are where the zones are: demand at the range low, supply at the range high. Trade the edges with the short 1:1 plan, and leave the middle alone.

The steps.

  1. The ITF trend is NEUTRAL (Module 4.3). That makes it a sideways trade.
  2. Mark the demand zone at the range low and the supply zone at the range high. Score them; the far side of the range is the opposing zone, and it must be 3:1 or better.
  3. Bracket it at the near line in prep, stop below the zone. Write the 1:1 price on the order (Module 3.7).
  4. At 1:1: half off, stop to entry. The dream team rides to the far side of the range. With 1 futures contract: all out at 1:1.

What makes it work. In a range, the edges hold until they don't, so the plan takes money off early and lets only the risk-free half chase the full swing.

Where it fails. The range breaks. The stop below the zone handles it, and the break itself often leaves a fresh zone for a breakout retest. Middle-of-the-range trades are where sideways markets eat accounts: sit on your hands there.

Wyckoff calls this a range still being built. Wyckoff traders mostly wait for the spring or upthrust at an edge; we work both edges with the 1:1 plan instead. See Module 4.13.

Do it Note NEUTRAL trends and the range edges in Sunday prep.

Double bottom (reversal)

Under review · Counter-trend: a B trade, after Gate 1, at half risk.

The idea. The "W" everyone watches for is really a zone getting its first test. The first low builds a demand zone when price drops in, stalls and rallies out. The second bottom is price coming back to that zone. You don't wait for the W to finish; the order is already there.

The steps.

  1. A downtrend ends in a drop-base-rally at the first low: a reversal demand zone (Module 4.4). For a double top, mirror it: a rally-base-drop supply zone.
  2. Score it. It's against the ITF trend, so trend scores 0 and it tops out at 17: a B zone. It's tradable only after Gate 1, optional, at half risk (Module 7.10).
  3. Bracket it at the near line in prep, stop below the zone. The opposing zone must still be 3:1 or better.
  4. Counter-trend = the 1:1 plan: half off at 1:1, stop to entry, and the dream team rides to the opposing supply (Module 3.7).

What makes it work. The second bottom happens at a price where buyers already showed up once. Waiting for the neckline break, like most traders do, gives away most of the move and puts the stop far away.

Where it fails. The second bottom breaks through: the sellers weren't done. The stop below the zone handles it. Don't add to it at a third bottom.

Wyckoff calls this the secondary test of an accumulation's lows. When the first low pokes under the range and snaps back, it's a spring, and the second bottom is the test of that spring. See Module 4.13.

Do it Reversal zones go on the prep list as B zones, marked counter-trend, once Gate 1 is unlocked.
